# Submission FAQs

### Do the [quality guidelines](quality) have to be followed to the letter?

No. There is a lot of grey area and nuance in academic publishing. We could prescribe that papers must be from a top conference, but some of the best materials we've covered have been from outside these venues.

Even so, if you stray outside the recommendations given, you should have a good reason (expect to be asked). The more you diverge from the norm, the better your justification should be.

### Are pre-prints allowed?

Yes. But.

It is significantly harder to assess the quality of a paper before it has gone through peer review. There is no venue reputation to go off of and tools like the <a href="https://www.irit.fr/~Guillaume.Cabanac/problematic-paper-screener" target="_blank">Problematic Paper Screener</a> only check published papers. However, some pre-print servers (like arXiv) do assign DOIs, so you may still be able to check for comments on <a href="https://pubpeer.com/" target="_blank">PubPeer</a>.

If a pre-print is authored by a significant name in the field, you may use this as a rough stand-in for venue reputation.

For pre-prints to which that doesn't apply, you are expected to go beyond the usual checks. You should be reading and assessing a significant portion of the paper prior to nominating.

### How about grey literature?

Everything said about pre-prints above applies. It is even slightly harder to rely on simple checks for grey literature as usually these types of sources won't even have a DOI.

You should make an attempt to assess the reputation of the organisation which has authored or commissioned the report. And you must assess the text yourself directly, as with a pre-print.

### What about books?

A chapter from a book is, of course, acceptable. As it happens, many of the indicators of a paper quality also apply to books sold by academic publishers. Individual chapters even have their own DOIs assigned many times and are also evaluated by the <a href="https://www.irit.fr/~Guillaume.Cabanac/problematic-paper-screener" target="_blank">Problematic Paper Screener</a>.

Books published by non-academic publishers are also okay. But you will need to assess their quality more carefully. Don't assume that just because a publisher has paid someone to produce a manuscript it will be free of quality issues. Not all editors are equally good at their jobs.

### How do I find the DOI of a paper?

Look for a series of characters starting with &ldquo;10.&rdquo; and containing at least one forward slash. For example: &ldquo;10.1109/sp61157.2025.00076&rdquo;.

The location varies by publisher. Often it will be near the top of the metadata displayed on the publisher's website, just before or in the vicinity the abstract. Other times, it will be at the very bottom of the page, or at the start of the list of references.

If you don't see it on page, some publishers include it in the URL. The most consistent way of finding it, however, is to click the &ldquo;cite&rdquo; button on an article. The citation generated should include it, if the publisher assigns DOIs.

Unfortunately, some do not. The most significant ones in security which don't assign DOIs are:

- USENIX
- IACR Cryptology ePrint Archive

### What should I do if a paper doesn't have a DOI?

This makes automated checks difficult or impossible. But you can still manually check the venue's reputation (check the <a href="https://kanalregister.hkdir.no/en" target="_blank">Norwegian Register</a>, the <a href="https://jfp.csc.fi/jufoportal" target="_blank">Finnish Publication Forum</a>, <a href="https://portal.core.edu.au/conf-ranks/?search=4604&by=all&source=ICORE2026&sort=arank&page=1" target="_blank">ICORE</a>, and <a href="https://www.scimagojr.com/journalrank.php?area=1700" target="_blank">SCImago</a>), the reputation of the publisher (check <a href="https://beallslist.net/" target="_blank">Beall's List</a>), and the reputation of the authors. You can also look up the authors' other papers which do have DOIs and evaluate these.

As always, you should assess the contents of the paper yourself as well.

### What can I do if I come across an example of academic misconduct?

Unfortunately, your options are somewhat limited.

Your best recourse may be to leave a comment on <a href="https://pubpeer.com/" target="_blank">PubPeer</a> with your findings. This is a worthwhile public service to engage in to alert others in the research community.

However, you should be mindful that criticising another's work can draw unwanted attention to yourself. For this reason, it is suggested you post anonymously. You can do so by choosing the *Anonymous Registration* option when registering for a PubPeer account.

Elisabeth Bik has a <a href="https://scienceintegritydigest.com/2019/07/16/pubpeer-a-website-to-comment-on-scientific-papers/" target="_blank">good guide</a> to posting on PubPeer.
